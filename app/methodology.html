Methodology

How this was built, and what it does with your criteria

UpstreamIt reads companies' own hiring APIs instead of a job board's index of them. Here is where the jobs come from, what gets inferred from them, and what happens to a posting that says nothing about something you filtered on. The numbers marked live are read from the corpus as this page loads.

The premise

Almost every company runs its careers page on an applicant tracking system: Ashby, Greenhouse, Lever, Workday and a dozen others. Each publishes its boards over a public, unauthenticated API, so if you know a company's slug — the short name in jobs.ashbyhq.com/<slug> — you can pull its whole open req list in one request. A job board sits downstream of that, re-indexing the same postings on its own schedule, ranking them by criteria it does not publish, and selling placement in that ranking.

So the whole design is one move: go to the source, take everything, and put the filtering on your side of the wire instead of theirs.

The cost is worth stating up front. You get every company using an ATS we have an adapter for, and no other company at all. A firm on iCIMS, on BambooHR, or on a careers page it wrote itself is not ranked low here. It is absent.

The pipeline

Seven stages, each a separate program writing to one SQLite database and each re-runnable alone — so improving a rule about seniority re-runs stage four over data already on disk instead of re-crawling every board.

  1. 1
    Collect. Gather candidate slugs from public lists. Names that might be boards.
  2. 2
    Verify. Ask each ATS whether the slug is a real board. Live, empty or dead, with a date.
  3. 3
    Sweep. Pull every open posting from every live board, daily. The corpus, plus a log of what changed.
  4. 4
    Derive. Turn free text into columns a filter can reason about. Location, salary, seniority, skills, degree, clearance, sponsorship. No network, no model.
  5. 5
    Read the company. One model call per company over its own postings. What it does, in a sentence and one of thirty sectors.
  6. 6
    Filter. Run your criteria over those columns, in memory. Every criterion returns match, no, or unknown.
  7. 7
    Rank and show. Order what survived, and say why it is there. A link straight to the company's own posting.

Finding the boards

No ATS publishes a directory of its customers. Ashby has no sitemap, its collection endpoints return 401, and every board is a path on one shared host, so there is nothing in DNS to enumerate. The slugs are assembled from eleven public datasets, a web-archive crawl and a manual file — and they saturate fast: counting only the live boards a source contributed that no other source had, the crawl added 113, the next best 45, then 11, 7, 3, 2, 1, 1, with the best-known repo adding zero.

A collected slug is a claim until it is probed. Every one is asked of the live API and sorted into live, empty or dead, with a date. The empty ones are kept: a company not hiring this month will hire again, and discarding it means re-discovering it from a source that may no longer list it.

Sweeping daily

Every live board is pulled once a day. The ATSes read today are Ashby, Greenhouse, Lever and Workday, each through an adapter that turns that vendor's payload into the same shape as the others. Every posting carries a content hash and every observation goes into an event log, which is what makes it possible to say which jobs are new, which were edited without announcement, and which stopped being listed. Storing only the current state tells you what is open, never what moved while you were not looking.

Age is not evidence. Nothing is pruned for being old — a posting a company still publishes is still open whatever its date says, and 13.8% of live postings are over a year old. Date posted is a filter you can apply, never one applied for you.

Turning prose into columns

An ATS payload gives you a title, a description and a few unreliable enums. It does not give you "entry level in New York paying over $90k". Every criterion is inferred here, by rules, touching no network. Four calls carry most of the weight:

The problemThe call
A posting states several experience requirements. 15.8% state more than one figure, disagreeing 82.5% of the time. The strictest wins. The error is asymmetric: a junior job you miss is invisible, a flood of senior ones makes the list useless.
The salary interval field lies. 154 rows carried "per year" under $1,000; 54 exceeded $1M. Each figure is re-read under whichever interval makes it plausible. If none does, the job is salary unknown rather than carrying a wrong number into a filter.
Locations are as often addresses as city names. Parsing loosely minted metros out of building names: one Korean office block became three "metros". An unrecognised place yields no metro rather than a guess. Rejects go to a report, the to-do list for the alias table.
No ATS has a seniority field, so it comes from the title and the description, which disagree. Both must agree. Years alone lets Senior Engineer through whenever the description states no number (44.1% of postings); the title alone lets Associate Consultant, 8+ years required.

What the company does

A posting says what the job is, not what the company is — and a data engineer at a bank and one at a payments startup are the same posting and different careers. No ATS has a field for it, so a language model reads the "about us" paragraph and the open roles once per company, storing a sentence and one of thirty sectors. It is the only column that came from a model rather than a rule, and the detail pane says which model, how sure it was, and when.

Unsure is stored as unknown, not as a guess. "Not finance" drops a company the model read as a bank; it never drops one whose postings did not say. The sector is known for — of open jobs across — live boards.

When a posting says nothing

A filter may rule a job out because the posting says something that fails it. Never because the posting is silent.

That is the central design decision here. Every criterion returns match, no, or unknown — and for several criteria unknown is the most common answer.

CriterionSilentWhat happens to the silent ones by default
Salary74.1%Kept. A silent salary floor would hide three quarters of the market and still look like a working search.
Degree required61.6%Kept. Three postings in five never mention school.
Visa sponsorship96.8%Kept. "Only companies that say they sponsor" is a very narrow gate, and it is labelled as one.
Seniority24.9%Shown separately, in a "worth a look" list. Too many to drop, too many to mix in.
Location15.9%Dropped, the one exception: a job with no parseable location is not plausibly in your city. Still a switch you can flip.
Company sector—Kept. Companies nobody has read yet, or the model would not commit on.
Workplace, type, date posted≤ 1.1%Kept. Small enough not to matter either way.

Each is a control on the filter rail, not a decision baked into the code, and each prints the share of the corpus you would drop by changing it. In a survey of sixty job boards, the closest anyone came was one "include jobs without salary" checkbox.

Filtering in SQLBuilt, measured, replaced. Every facet count has to leave one criterion out, so each dimension needs its own near-duplicate query. The hot columns live in memory instead, and one pass produces the results and every count together.

What this does not do

  • It sees four ATSes. Companies on iCIMS, BambooHR or a hand-built careers page are absent entirely.
  • It cannot tell you a company's size — no ATS publishes headcount. "Roles this company has open here" is a proxy, and is labelled as one.
  • It cannot tell you a posting is genuinely open, only that the company still publishes it — the strongest evidence available without asking the recruiter.
  • Inference has edges. Seniority, metro and skills are read out of prose by rules, the sector by a model — a staffing agency's board reads as the agency, whoever the roles are for. Each can return unknown, and unknown is never treated as a "no".

Where the numbers come from

The four figures at the top are read from the corpus as it loads; the rest come from the reports each stage writes on every run, and the rules behind them are covered by 989 checks that need no database and no network.
