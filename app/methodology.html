Methodology

How this was built, and what it does with your criteria

UpstreamIt reads companies' own hiring APIs instead of a job board's index of them. Here is where the jobs come from, what gets inferred from them, and what happens to a posting that says nothing about something you filtered on. The numbers marked live are read from the corpus as this page loads.

The premise

Almost every company runs its careers page on an applicant tracking system: Ashby, Greenhouse, Lever and a dozen others. That is where the recruiter types the posting, and where it disappears from when the role is filled. Each ATS publishes its boards over a public, free, unauthenticated API, so if you know a company's slug, the short name in jobs.ashbyhq.com/<slug>, you can pull its whole open req list in one request.

A job board sits downstream of that. It re-indexes the same postings on its own schedule, ranks them by criteria it does not publish, and usually sells placement in that ranking. The gap between what a company has open and what a board shows you is where stale listings and paid placement live.

So the whole design is one move: go to the source, take everything, and put the filtering on your side of the wire instead of theirs.

The cost of that is worth stating up front. You get every company using an ATS we have an adapter for, and no other company at all. A firm on Workday, on iCIMS, or on a careers page it wrote itself is not ranked low here. It is absent.

The pipeline

Seven stages. Each is a separate program writing to one SQLite database, and each can be re-run on its own, so improving a rule about how a job title maps to a seniority band re-runs stage four over data already on disk instead of re-crawling eleven thousand boards.

  1. 1
    Collect. Gather candidate company slugs from public lists. Output: names that might be boards.
  2. 2
    Verify. Ask each ATS whether that slug is a real board. Output: live, empty or dead, recorded with a date.
  3. 3
    Sweep. Pull every open posting from every live board, daily. Output: the corpus, plus a log of what changed.
  4. 4
    Derive. Turn free text into columns a filter can reason about. Location, salary, seniority, workplace, skills, degree, clearance, sponsorship. No network, no model.
  5. 5
    Read the company. One model call per company over its own postings. What it does, in a sentence and one of thirty sectors. The only stage that reads anything but an ATS.
  6. 6
    Filter. Run your criteria over those columns, in memory. Every criterion returns match, no, or unknown.
  7. 7
    Rank and show. Order what survived, and say why each one is there. Output: the page, and a link straight to the company's own posting.

Finding the boards

No ATS publishes a directory of its customers. Ashby has no sitemap, its collection endpoints return 401, and every board is a path on one shared host, so there is nothing in DNS to enumerate either. The slugs have to be assembled from elsewhere: eleven public datasets, a web-archive crawl, and a manual file for anything found by hand. A source that fails to answer keeps its prior claims, because one upstream outage should not read as thousands of companies going out of business overnight.

Knowing when to stop adding sources is the harder half. Counting the live boards each source contributed that no other source had, the web crawl added 113, the next best 45, and the tail ran 11, 7, 3, 2, 1, 1, with the largest and best-known repo adding zero. Web archives saturate about as fast: one month of Common Crawl found 2,820 slugs, and forty-nine months found 4,354.

A collected slug is only a claim until it is probed. Every one is asked of the live API and sorted into live (the board exists and has openings), empty (it exists, nobody is hiring right now) or dead (404), each stored with a date. Of the Ashby candidates, 7,951 slugs resolved to 4,297 confirmed boards and 3,654 confirmed-absent ones. The empty ones are kept: a company with no openings this month will hire again, and discarding it means re-discovering it later from a source that may no longer list it.

A failure mode found in production. A company can switch its hosted Ashby board off while its postings stay in the API. The posting still comes back, and the jobUrl in it now 404s, which looks perfectly good until you click it. Those boards are detected directly now, and their jobs link through the company's own careers page instead.

Sweeping daily

Every live board is pulled once a day. The ATSes read today are Ashby, Greenhouse and Lever, each through an adapter whose only job is turning that vendor's payload into the same shape as the others. They are not equivalent, so the app says which board a job came from rather than pretending otherwise.

Every posting is stored with a content hash, and every observation goes into an event log. That is what makes it possible to say which jobs are new since yesterday, which were edited without announcement (a salary band changed, a requirement added), and which stopped being listed. A tool that only stores the current state can tell you what is open. It cannot tell you what moved while you were not looking.

Age is not evidence. Nothing is pruned for being old. A posting a company still publishes is still open whatever its date says, and 13.8% of live postings are more than a year old right now. Date posted is a filter you can apply, never one applied on your behalf.

Turning prose into columns

An ATS payload gives you a title, a description and a handful of unreliable enums. It does not give you "entry level in New York paying over $90k". Every criterion in the app is inferred here, in a pass that touches no network and can be re-run whenever a rule improves. Four of those inferences carry most of the weight:

The problemThe call, and what it cost
A posting states several different experience requirements. 15.8% state more than one figure, and they disagree 82.5% of the time, with a median gap of three years. The strictest one wins. A posting wanting "5+ years in sales" and "1+ year with Salesforce" is a five-year job. Taking the smallest would have moved 23% more postings into the "two years or less" bucket, and the error is asymmetric: a junior job you miss is invisible to you, while a flood of senior ones makes the list useless.
The salary interval field lies. 154 rows carried "per year" with a value under $1,000, and 54 exceeded $1M. Every figure is re-read under whichever interval makes it plausible ($5k to $2M a year; a range whose top is more than 8× its bottom is refused outright, and a reading under an interval the posting did not state is capped at $1M). If none does, the job is recorded as salary unknown rather than carrying a confidently wrong number into a filter.
Location strings are as often full addresses as city names. Parsing them loosely minted metro areas out of building names: one Korean office block became three separate "metros" of 97 jobs each. An unrecognised place yields no metro rather than a guess. Fragments carrying digits, facility words, or more than four words are rejected and written to a report, which is the to-do list for the alias table.
No ATS has a seniority field, so it has to come from the title and the description, which disagree. Both have to agree. Years alone lets Senior Engineer through whenever the description states no number, which is 44.1% of postings; the title alone lets Associate Consultant, 8+ years required through. Requiring both costs a couple of dozen senior-titled postings that claim two years or less.

What the company does

A posting says what the job is. It does not say what the company is, and that is the thing most people look up before they apply: a data engineer at a bank and a data engineer at a payments startup are the same posting and different careers. No ATS has a field for it, so it is read the way a person would read it — from the "about us" paragraph nearly every description carries, and from the list of roles the company has open, which says what kind of organisation this is even when the prose does not.

That reading is done by a language model, once per company rather than once per job, and stored on the company: a one-sentence description and one of thirty sectors (fintech, healthcare providers, developer tools, defense, …). Every job on the board inherits its company's answer. It is the one column in the corpus that came from a model rather than a rule, and the detail pane says so on every job: which model, how sure it was, and when.

Unsure is stored as unknown, not as a guess. The model is asked how confident it is, and a low answer is thrown away. A company it could not place is treated exactly like a company nobody has read yet: unknown, kept in every search by default. So is "something else", unless you ask for it by name.

The sector filter has two halves and the second is the useful one. "Not finance" drops a company the model read as a bank; it does not drop the one whose postings never said, because that one has not been shown to be a bank. Right now the sector is known for — of open jobs across — live boards; the rest are unknown and stay in.

When a posting says nothing

A filter may rule a job out because the posting says something that fails it. Never because the posting is silent.

That is the central design decision here. Every criterion returns one of three answers rather than two: match, no, and unknown. Unknown is not rare. For several criteria it is the most common answer.

CriterionSilentWhat happens to the silent ones by default
Salary74.1%Kept. Only 25.9% of postings publish a figure at all. A silently applied salary floor would hide most of the market and still look like a working search.
Degree required61.6%Kept. Three postings in five never mention school.
Visa sponsorship96.8%Kept. The sparsest field in the corpus. "Only companies that say they sponsor" is a very narrow gate, and it is labelled as one.
Seniority24.9%Shown separately in a "worth a look" list beside the confirmed matches. Too many to drop, too many to mix in.
Location15.9%Dropped, the one deliberate exception. A job with no parseable location is not plausibly in your city, and keeping them would swamp any location filter. It is still a switch you can flip.
Workplace, employment type, date posted≤ 1.1%Kept. Small enough not to matter either way.
Company sector—Kept. The share of open jobs at companies nobody has read yet, or that the model would not commit on. Excluding a sector only ever removes a company the model placed in it.

Each of those is a control on the filter rail rather than a decision baked into the code, and each prints the percentage of the corpus you would drop by changing it. In a survey of sixty job boards, none had an equivalent. The closest anyone came was a single "include jobs without salary" checkbox.

What the interface has to do

  • Show a count on every control. Someone who picks four criteria blind and gets nothing needs to see which one was too narrow.
  • Build the controls from the data, so an option that would match nothing is never drawn, and a new city or a fourth ATS grows a control with no code change.
  • Link straight to the company's own posting, with no page in between.
  • Say when a number changed. Where duplicate postings are collapsed, the count says so: 453 dropping to 291 with no explanation looks the same as a broken filter.
  • Sort without discarding. Sorting by salary sinks the jobs with no figure rather than deleting them.

Tried and rejected

These are measured results, written down so they do not get re-attempted:

ApproachResult
Ashby sitemap, robots, or a public directoryDoes not exist. Collection endpoints return 401.
Search-engine enumeration for board URLsOne engine serves a CAPTCHA, one returns zero results for the host, one blocks outright. Zero slugs per page.
Technology-detection services (BuiltWith, Wappalyzer)Neither tracks Ashby at all, checked against the full 7,278-technology catalogue.
Guessing slugs from a startup directory75 companies produced 125 guesses and exactly one hit.
Buying a vendor dataset (~$400)Its headline company count counts posting mentions, not boards. Heavily inflated.
Filtering in SQLBuilt, measured, replaced. Facet counts have to leave one criterion out, so every dimension needs its own near-duplicate query, and the title matcher would have had to exist twice. The hot columns are held in memory instead, and one pass produces the results and every facet count together.

One avenue was rejected on safety rather than yield: a repository claiming to list Ashby boards ships an archive containing an executable pair in the shape commonly used by malware loaders. It was not downloaded and is not wired in.

What this does not do

  • It sees three ATSes. Companies hiring through Workday, iCIMS, BambooHR or a hand-built careers page are absent entirely.
  • It cannot tell you a company's size, because no ATS publishes headcount. The app offers "roles this company has open here", which is a proxy, and labels it as one.
  • The sector is a model's reading of the company's own words, not a registry lookup. A staffing agency's board is the agency; a job aggregator's board is the aggregator, whoever the roles are really for. The detail pane names the model and its confidence so a wrong one can be traced rather than trusted.
  • It cannot tell you a posting is genuinely open, only that the company still publishes it. That is the strongest evidence available without asking the recruiter, and postings do linger.
  • Inference has edges. Seniority, metro and skills are read out of prose by rules, and every one of those rules can return unknown, which is treated as unknown rather than as a "no".
  • The public slug lists are exhausted, not complete. Some boards nobody has published a pointer to are not reachable by collecting more lists.

Where the numbers come from

The four figures at the top of this page are read from the corpus as it loads. The rest come from the reports each stage writes on every run, and the derivation and filtering rules behind them are covered by 931 checks that need no database and no network. Where a figure here is a snapshot rather than a live read, it says so.
